Matchup 1: Finn Allen vs Vipraj Nigam

This is the matchup the chase pivoted on. Allen had already hit Vipraj Nigam's first over for 12 from four balls in the 7th — a warm-up. His second over, the 13th, cost 19 runs: six, six, six — three consecutive on a stand-and-deliver line that stripped any spinner-vs-power-hitter calculus. Aggregate: 31 off 8 balls. Strike rate 388 against Allen's overall 213. Overall is already absurd — this matchup is multiples beyond it.

Why did the captain keep going back? Match-ups are made for left-arm wrist-spinners against right-handed launch-pad batters; Nigam should have been a defensive option. Instead, every fuller ball got hammered into the long-on stand and every shorter one got pulled. Should KKR persist with this matchup in future games? They should hope DC do.

Matchup 2: Finn Allen vs Kuldeep Yadav

Kuldeep Yadav is one of T20's most economical wrist-spinners. On this night, he wasn't. 32 runs off 14 balls — a strike rate of 229 versus Allen's overall 213. Four sixes from the matchup: 7.4, 11.1, 13.2 and 13.5. Allen treated the leg-spinner like a length bowler at the death.

Was it bad bowling? Mostly, no — Kuldeep stuck to his stock ball, hit his lengths. The problem is Allen had decided the chase by then. Once the win probability is above 95%, every ball becomes an asymmetric bet for the batter, and Kuldeep's variation didn't reset that asymmetry. In a tighter game, this matchup looks different. Tonight it looked like batting practice.

Matchup 3: Finn Allen vs Lungi Ngidi

The exception. Ngidi bowled 2-0-7-0 across the chase and Allen managed 3 off 6 against him — a strike rate of 50 against an overall of 213. That ratio is the single piece of evidence that Allen could be contained on this surface, this night, by the right bowler with the right plan.

Hard, into the pitch, around the stumps; Ngidi gave Allen nothing to free his arms on. The captain should have given him another over. That's the painful counterfactual: DC's only effective spell got cut off at 12 balls. Should Axar Patel double down on this matchup next time? Yes — and start him in the powerplay, not the middle.

Matchup 4: Pathum Nissanka vs Anukul Roy

Critechery's eyes were on this matchup the moment the team sheets came out. Nissanka — three sixes already, fifty in 29 — had taken Roy for 16 runs off 8 balls, strike rate 200 against his overall 172. Then the punchline: Roy got him stumped at 10.2, on a delivery that was actually called wide. A wide that ended in a stumping is the cricket equivalent of a foul that becomes a turnover. Nissanka came down the pitch, Roy bowled it shorter and slower, the spin took the bat out of the equation, and Raghuvanshi had time to whip the bails off.

For three balls in the same over Roy then bowled Stubbs through the gate. Two wickets. Three balls. From 80/3 to 89/5. DC never recovered to a winning total because of one over from one bowler — and the matchup that flipped wasn't a planned plan, it was a spinner who held his nerve when his most-attacked batter walked at him.

Turning Points

Two innings, three squeezes — and only one team was holding the rope.

Turning Point 1 — Roy's 11th over

DC were 80/3 going into the 11th. Pathum Nissanka — five fours, three sixes, on for the kind of innings that anchors a 180 — danced down the pitch on the second ball. Anukul Roy went shorter, wider, slower. The ball was called wide. It was also a stumping. Three balls later, Tristan Stubbs went bowled through the gate trying to punch a 95kph length ball to cover. Two wickets in the same over from a left-arm fingerspinner with a knack for moving the ball after pitching. DC went from 80/3 to 89/5 and never threatened the kind of par that Allen would later treat as a starting line.

Turning Point 2 — Allen's 7th over assault

The chase was 43/2 after the powerplay, asking rate 6.7. Vipraj Nigam came on for his first over. Allen took 12 off four balls — 1, dot, 4, 6, 1, dot. The three boundaries reset the equation: the run rate stayed comfortably under 8 even as Nigam was bowling, which meant Allen could afford to keep going hard. The matchup made the rest of the chase available.

Turning Point 3 — Allen's three sixes off Nigam in the 13th

By the time KKR were 122/2 in the 13th, the chase was already a formality. But the third sixes — the trio Allen launched into the deep midwicket stands off Nigam at 12.2, 12.3, 12.4 — was the moment the match crossed from formality to spectacle. Allen reached the 80s. The crowd stood up. The stadium realised it was watching a hundred. Pressure on this delivery was 0.65 — high — but pressure on the bowler, not the batter.

The Inflexion Point

Pick any one moment to label the match-defining ball: every choice tells the same story. Critechery's pressure-and-probability blend points to over 6.4, when Finn Allen launched Vipraj Nigam's third ball over deep midwicket for six.

Before that ball, KKR's win probability was 0.85. After it: 0.92. A small jump on the surface — but the slope was the entire match in miniature. Allen had been picking ones, finding Starc and Axar early, surviving Ngidi. Then Nigam came on. The first ball — single. The second — dot. The third — four. The fourth — six. From that ball, the chase stopped feeling like a chase. It became Allen's century in real-time, with KKR carrying him to 14.2 overs.

Ball 6.4 — Allen vs NigamLength on the stumps, sat up to be hit, and Allen swings clean. Disappears into the deep midwicket stand. Nigam looks down at his hand for an answer that isn't there.

Once that ball goes, the rest of the chase is calibration: how many sixes can Allen hit before someone runs out of bowlers willing to bowl to him? Answer: ten.

That Allen Over

The 13th over of the KKR chase — Vipraj Nigam's second and last — has to be the over they show on the highlight reel ten years from now. It cost 16 runs, with three consecutive sixes from Allen at 12.2, 12.3 and 12.4. Each one cleared the rope by a cricket pitch. The second one took Allen to 73, and the third — into a noise that wasn't celebration but disbelief — took him to 79.

Allen hit ten sixes in his innings. Three of them came in this over. The other seven were spread across nine other overs. By the time Nigam finished his fourth and final ball of the over, the camera kept cutting to the dugout where Allen's teammates were already half out of their seats; Mitchell Starc — who'd just bowled a wicketless 3-0-31-0 — was watching with the resigned look of a fast bowler who knows the spin he was depending on isn't coming.

Ball 12.4 — Allen vs NigamTossed up, on a fuller length. Allen reads it before it leaves the hand, comes down the track, and absolutely murders it over long-on. The crowd doesn't sit down for the next two minutes.

The interesting question for DC isn't whether Nigam should have bowled this over — at that point, Axar Patel had run out of better options. It's whether Lungi Ngidi (who'd gone 2-0-7-0 to that point) should have been kept back to bowl at Allen specifically. Ngidi had given Allen 3 off 6 balls. He was the one bowler who'd held him. Instead, his quota ended at 2 overs. Saving Ngidi for the death assumed there'd be a death. There wasn't.

How the Innings Burned

The Burn Rate chart shows two innings making completely different bargains with their wickets. DC's path is jagged: powerplay survival (one wicket), then a full-on collapse — five wickets between overs 8 and 11 — then a death-overs scramble that wasn't enough to push past par. By Critechery's classification, three of DC's middle overs were Reckless (run rate under 8 with multiple wickets falling). The death phase recovered Efficient classification at best, with Ashutosh's 39 from 28 balls keeping the score from collapsing entirely.

KKR's chase looks completely different. They lost two wickets in the powerplay (Rahane to a non-striker run-out, Raghuvanshi bowled by Axar) — but those wickets weren't reckless, they were the cost of an aggressive opening that stayed under control. The middle phase is the cleanest Burn Rate Critechery has tracked all season: 104 runs in 9 overs without losing a wicket. Eight of those nine overs sit in Critechery's Attacking zone (run rate over 10, no wickets fallen). If DC's burn was a forest fire, KKR's was a controlled detonation.

The Architecture

This chart only really tells one story: Allen and Green's unbroken 116 off 64 balls is the load-bearing partnership of the night, the season, and probably the early IPL. Strike rate 181. Two batters who barely needed to talk to each other — Allen launching, Green rotating, both knowing that the chase was already won and that the only thing left to do was how. This wasn't a partnership; it was a chaperone. Green's job in the second half of the chase was to give Allen the strike. He ran 33 off 27 with two sixes, perfectly timed to keep Nigam and Kuldeep within Allen's reach.

DC's biggest stand — Nissanka and Rahul's 49 off 30 in the powerplay — would have been the load-bearer in a winning innings. Instead it became damage limitation in retrospect: the only DC partnership that resembled a partnership before the middle-order collapsed. The Ashutosh–Axar 39 off 46 was the rescue, but rescue at run rate 5.1 in the middle of a chase target negotiation is too slow for the brief.

Counterfactual: if the Nissanka–Rahul stand had carried into the 8th over instead of breaking at 4.6, DC has a base. They didn't. They scrambled.
